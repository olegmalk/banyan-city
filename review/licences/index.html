Written for you, 2026‑08‑10. Two decisions are waiting on you here: D15 (the stills we drew episode 1 from) and D16 (LTX, the video model). Neither of us is a lawyer and I say so again at the end, with a list of everything I could not settle.

1. What I got wrong

Correction

I told you the licence on our stills contained two rules: that nobody may train another AI model on our pictures, and that we may not present the work as human‑made.

Neither sentence is in that licence. I searched the full text for them. They are not there in any wording.

Where they came from: our own lint tool prints a line describing Google Flow — a different licence entirely, sitting on three archived files that have nothing to do with the stills. I read our tool's description of Flow and attached it to animagine, the model that drew the stills. Then I built the whole D15 argument on top of it.

This matters more than a normal mistake would, because those two invented rules were doing real work. "You cannot present it as human‑made" is the kind of clause that would reach our TikTok posts and our own website. It would have been a genuine constraint on you. It does not exist.

Everything below is read from the licence text itself, not from our notes about it.

2. What the stills licence actually says

The model that drew all fifteen approved stills is cagliostrolab/animagine-xl-3.1. Its licence is CreativeML Open RAIL++‑M — the same document Stability publishes with Stable Diffusion XL, which is where I read it from.

The parts that are plainly good for us

"Except as set forth herein, Licensor claims no rights in the Output You generate using the Model."

  • The frames are ours. Nobody upstream claims them.
  • Commercial use is permitted.
  • No credit is required. No line saying which model drew it, anywhere.
  • No AI‑disclosure is required. Nothing in this licence asks us to label a picture as machine‑generated.
  • Nothing bars presenting the work as our own. This is the invented rule from section 1, and its absence is the single biggest change to the picture.

The duties that do exist attach to the model file, not to a video

The licence has real obligations — pass the terms along, include the restrictions, give notice. Every one of them is triggered by "Distribution", and Distribution is a defined term in the document: it covers handing on the Model, or Derivatives of the Model. Publishing a video is not Distribution under this licence, and the section containing those duties imposes nothing on it.

The obvious follow‑up is whether our film counts as a "Derivative of the Model". It does not. I read that definition branch by branch and every branch ends in another model — a modification of the weights, a fine‑tune, a model transferring the original's behaviour. A LoRA trained on our stills would be a Derivative. An episode is not.

The restriction list, and the one sentence that is live

The licence carries Attachment A: eleven prohibited uses. Its opening line is "You agree not to use the Model or Derivatives of the Model" — grammatically, it is about using the model. Nothing on the list touches publishing an animated short unless the film's own content does one of the listed things: defaming or harassing people, exploiting minors, generating harmful disinformation, unlawful discrimination, and so on. Ours does none of them.

The one clause that reaches our output

Section 6, third sentence: "No use of the output can contravene any provision as stated in the License." The natural reading is that the film's content must not itself do the Attachment A things. On that reading we already comply, and always have.

Genuinely unresolved — and I want to be blunt about it

Whether that sentence binds people who merely watch or reshare our video is not answered by the licence. It never spells out a mechanism — how a rule would attach to a stranger who was never given the model and never agreed to anything.

I checked the two places you would expect an answer. The RAIL Initiative's own FAQ and Hugging Face's OpenRAIL explainer never use the word "output" at all. Every claim online that the restrictions follow the pictures downstream to viewers is somebody's extrapolation from that one cross‑reference. It is not text.

Two side facts worth having

  • OpenRAIL‑M and OpenRAIL++‑M differ in exactly one working way. The "++" version deleted a duty to use the latest version of the model. Attachment A is word‑for‑word identical between them. So if you see either name in our notes, the restriction list is the same list.
  • Animagine's licence was switched on 2025‑12‑22, by a one‑line commit. Before that date it was the Fair AI Public License 1.0‑SD. Any note in our repo written earlier names the old one, and that is not a contradiction, it is a date. Under both licences, publishing a film was unencumbered — the old one's extra duties were also about modified models and source code.

One citation caveat: the LICENSE.md Stability publishes has lost its clause numbers along the way. It cross‑references "paragraph 5" but shows no digits anywhere. I have quoted sentences rather than numbers for that reason.

3. Where D15 stands now

D15's original argument had two halves. Both are gone, for different reasons.

The halfWhat happened to it
Pass‑through. Our site offers everything under CC BY 4.0, which grants strangers unrestricted reuse. We cannot grant rights we do not hold. Removed on 2026‑08‑04 by dad's watch‑only call — "when we publish clips they are for people to watch, not use for anything." With no reuse offer on the media, there is nothing to pass through.
The two extra clauses I said the licence contained. They were never in it. Section 1 above.
So the honest question has changed

It is no longer "the restrictions travel, so we cannot offer this under CC BY 4.0." It is now: does our own gate refuse more than the licence requires?

What is settled by the text

Nothing in OpenRAIL++‑M stops us publishing an animated short made from animagine stills — on banyan.city, on TikTok, commercially, without credit and without an AI label — provided the film's own content is not one of the eleven prohibited things.

What is genuinely ambiguous

Whether the restrictions reach the people who watch it. Under watch‑only this may not matter to us at all, because we are not granting viewers anything in the first place. But "probably does not matter" is not the same as "answered", and it is not answered.

What that means for the nine beats

Measured against the live gate on 2026‑08‑10, not remembered: nine of the fifteen beats refuse — 03, 06, 07, 08, 09, 10, 12, 14, 15 — and all nine refuse for that one reason, the OpenRAIL++ classification. The composite cut reports only the first one it hits, which is why error messages name beat 03 alone.

  • Beats 01, 02, 11 and 13 pass on their own records.
  • Beats 04 and 05 pass by silence, not by clearance. Their records say model: none and point at stills that have no provenance file beside them at all. The gate reads unprovenanced as permitted, on purpose. If anyone writes an honest record naming animagine, the refusals go from nine to eleven. Writing that record is your call, not a lane's.
The recommendation I am allowed to make, and the line I did not cross

Our own decision record already says the gate is stricter than we now need. D16's item 3 puts it plainly: licence_gate.py still implements the old test — "does the grant reach the output" — which is "stricter than we now need but never wrong in the dangerous direction."

I think the gate over‑refuses these nine beats. I did not change it. Reclassifying animagine is a governance call and it is yours; a steward quietly relaxing the one gate that can stop an unpublishable episode is exactly the failure the gate exists to prevent.

One thing D15 does not reach

D13 blocks five beats of the live episode for a completely different reason: PixVerse's free tier is personal‑use‑only, and that binds us, the people rendering and posting. Watch‑only does not clear it and neither does anything on this page. Keep the two apart — deciding D15 does not unblock D13.

4. D16 — LTX, the video model

Same treatment, read from the LTX‑2 Community Licence itself.

The parts that are good for us

  • Free under $10M of annual company revenue. The threshold gates using the model, not what we do with the output. We are nowhere near it.
  • "Licensor claims no rights in the Output you generate using LTX‑2." The footage is ours.
  • No credit required — and section 8 actually forbids implying that Lightricks endorses us.
  • The pass‑along duties attach to distributing the model or a derivative — weights, a LoRA, a model trained on our outputs. Publishing a video triggers none of them.

The one real obligation

Attachment A, item 5

Wherever the content is posted, it must "expressly and intelligibly" disclaim that it is machine generated.

The licence does not say what form this takes. A line in the caption, a card on screen, or TikTok's own "AI‑generated" toggle could each plausibly satisfy it. That is genuinely unspecified, and choosing the form is a decision, not a lookup.

Two more that stay live:

  • Item 7 — no impersonating real people without their consent.
  • Item 20 — no product that "directly competes with Lightricks' offerings." Undefined and ambiguous. This is the clause behind our standing rule that a contributor‑facing render service is the one thing LTX must never power, and it is the open question you were already being asked about.

An important difference from the older LTX licence: the restrictions now follow the Outputs, not just the model. That constrains what we do with our own footage. It does not force us to bind viewers.

Is D16 independent of D15?

Yes. Different models, different licences, different beats. Deciding D15 does not decide D16, and vice versa. The only thing they share is the direction of travel: both licences turn out to say considerably less about published video than we had assumed.

A defect on our side, and a concrete fix

The repo we actually load weights from, diffusers/LTX-2.3-Distilled-Diffusers, has no LICENSE file at all, and names a licence document that does not exist (ltx-video-2-open-source-license). Its link happens to resolve to the right text, so nothing is actually mis‑licensed — but our provenance cites a document by a name nothing serves, which is the kind of thing that reads as diligence and is not.

Recommendation: cite Lightricks/LTX-2.3/raw/main/LICENSE instead. It is a record fix, not a decision. I have not made it, because it touches the gate's tables and I was told to keep out of them.

5. Your options

These are laid out flat. I am not steering you toward one, and I have said where I think the text points rather than what you should do about it.

For D15 (the stills)

A. Change nothing.

The gate keeps refusing nine beats. Episode 1 stays where it is. You revisit whenever you want to.

This is a real option, not a null one. The gate erring conservative is defensible: you are fourteen, publishing under your dad's oversight, and "we could probably publish this but we are choosing not to yet" is a legitimate answer that costs nothing irreversible. The work does not disappear; it waits.

Cost: episode 1 does not go to canon. The nine beats stay unshowable on the public site. No re-renders, no risk.

B. Decide the licence permits publication, and let the gate say so.

You record that OpenRAIL++'s restrictions are about using the model and about the film's own content, that our film complies, and that the gate should stop treating animagine as unpublishable. Someone then reclassifies it in licence_gate.py.

Cost: a judgement call about a genuine ambiguity, made without a lawyer. If it is wrong, the exposure is that we published a film whose upstream terms someone reads more broadly than we did — there is no money and no contract in play, but it would be a public position we would have to walk back. Gets you: nine beats clear at once.

C. Narrow what we offer, in writing, generally.

This is D15's original "way out 1", and we have already done it for one surface — the review gallery images publish under a narrowed offer rather than CC BY 4.0. You could extend that to all rendered media: text, scripts, node writing, taste files, pipeline and the repo stay open exactly as they are; only the mp4s come out of the reuse offer.

Cost: needs an amendment to D1 (our content licence) and a licence file that says so — a change to a resolved governance decision, which is yours to make. No re-renders. Gets you: the ambiguity stops mattering, because we would no longer be promising anyone anything we might not hold.

D. Re-draw the fifteen stills on a model whose grant we can pass on freely.

Removes the question instead of answering it.

Cost: a full still pass on fifteen compositions, a new approval round from you on all fifteen, and the look will shift — these are the stills the current style was built on. Realistically days of loop, not hours, and no candidate model has been surveyed yet. Gets you: certainty, at the price of the pictures you already approved.

For D16 (LTX) — decided separately

E. Answer item 20 and pick the label form.

Two things in one: say that we will never build a contributor‑facing render service powered by LTX (which is the standing rule already, it just needs your word), and choose how we satisfy the machine‑generated disclaimer — caption line, on‑screen card, or platform toggle.

Cost: one sentence of policy plus one label per post, forever. Gets you: the v35 cut and the sixteen re‑draws become showable.

F. Leave LTX withheld.

The footage exists and stays unpublished. Nothing breaks.

Cost: a whole cut of episode 1 and today's sixteen clips stay invisible outside this machine.

7. Everything I do not know

Listed as unknown, not softened into "probably fine".

  1. Whether OpenRAIL++'s restrictions bind viewers and resharers. The licence gives no mechanism. Its own drafters' FAQ does not address output at all. This is the central ambiguity and it is unresolved by the document itself.
  2. Whether "no use of the output can contravene any provision" means the film's content, or something broader. I have given you the natural reading. I cannot give you the authoritative one.
  3. What form LTX's "expressly and intelligibly" disclaimer must take. Unspecified in the licence. Caption, card and platform toggle are all plausible; none is confirmed.
  4. What "directly competes with Lightricks' offerings" covers (LTX item 20). Undefined in the text. Our standing rule is a cautious guess at its edge, not a reading of it.
  5. Whether any court or the licence stewards have ever tested any of this. I found no such thing, and absence of a finding is not a finding of absence.
  6. Neither of us is a lawyer. This is a careful reading of two documents by someone who is not qualified to give you legal advice, and everything above should be weighted accordingly. If a decision here ever starts carrying real money, that is the point to get a real opinion.
